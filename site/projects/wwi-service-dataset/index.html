The research question

Does wartime sacrifice strengthen identification with the state, or lead to political disillusionment? My contribution was the data-engineering foundation: reconstructing historical service records so the research team could study those questions. The extraction work does not establish a causal finding.

What I built

  1. Document extraction. Rendered roster PDFs, used OCR and positioned text where available, and developed layout-aware extraction to keep each soldier's identity paired with the correct service history. The core schema captures name, residence, recorded race, serial number, and service history.
  2. Validation and recovery. Added row-count and column-shift checks, page-level checkpoints, and selective vision-model fallback for pages that failed structural validation. Source-linked corrections handled unusual serial numbers, missing text, and damaged records without repeating completed work.
  3. Casualty annotation. Preserved evidence quotes, volume and page references, and review flags while separating terminal-death labels from wounded-only or uncertain records.
  4. Advisor review. Built an editable workbook with filters, frozen headings, decision fields, and 402 priority cases. Deduplicated numeric serial numbers while retaining placeholder serials separately and preserving every collapsed source reference.

Why source review matters

On Volume 14's final roster page, a library stamp was mistaken for an extra soldier row. Checking the scan established that the page contained 12 soldiers, not 13; part of the final address remained illegible. The correction preserved the uncertainty and its source rather than filling the gap with a guess.

The Ohio pipeline processed 249,597 roster records across 23 volumes. It produced 6,749 death-marked records, reduced to 6,679 review rows by numeric-serial deduplication. These outputs still require human review of transcription, classification, page mapping, and conflicting records before publication.
